\begin{abstract}
Automated hiring systems overwhelmingly emit a score: a scalar that ranks a
candidate against others. Such scores resist explanation, give a candidate
nothing specific to contest, and carry growing regulatory exposure under New
York City Local Law~144, the Illinois Human Rights Act as amended by HB~3773,
and the EU Artificial Intelligence Act. We present CogniHire, a technical
screening system designed to audit the claims a candidate makes rather than
score the candidate. A deterministic grounding gate constrains a language model to select
claim text from the r\'esum\'e and forbids it to author any. Across roughly
100,000 synthetic trials the gate rejected every meaning-preserving paraphrase
and negation trap while admitting every verbatim claim, and under a worst-case
prompt-injection model it admitted none of 1,800 fabricated claims. For machine
learning we compare nine classifiers under grouped cross-validation with
significance testing and introduce a within-query diagnostic that separates
matching ability from candidate-level quality. For deep learning we show that a
plausible authored face-similarity threshold of 0.50 would have falsely rejected
41.4\% of genuine identity matches, against 3.4\% at a threshold calibrated on
Labeled Faces in the Wild. Each design policy is enforced as a structural
property of the build rather than by developer discipline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A technical interview ends and a form is filled in: ``Strong communication.
Good Flutter knowledge. 8.7/10.'' Six months later the number is indefensible.
Nobody can reconstruct which answer moved it from 8.4 to 8.7; the candidate
cannot appeal it and the recruiter cannot defend it. The number was a
compression of knowledge that discarded everything needed to audit it.

Automated interview platforms largely reproduce this failure at scale
\cite{bogen2018}. Faced with criticism, the industry has made the scalar more
sophisticated---more features, better models, fairness
post-processing~\cite{raghavan2020}---but few have asked whether a scalar was the
right output object at all.

We take the opposite position: the useful output of automated screening is an
audit of the claims a candidate has made, not a judgment of the candidate. A
r\'esum\'e is a set of assertions. Each can be probed, each probe yields
evidence, and evidence supports a verdict about the claim. The verdicts, with
evidence attached, go to a human who decides about the person. Grounding
governs what may be asserted, machine learning (ML) measures properties of
evidence, and deep learning (DL) supplies perception.

The philosophy of auditing rather than scoring is a design stance, not the
technical novelty. The novel mechanisms are specific and checkable:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An assertion-scoped grounding gate: a claim is admitted only as a
  verbatim, single-clause span of the r\'esum\'e whose clause carries no
  negation or hedge, and the gate returns the span's character offsets
  (Section~\ref{sec:grounding}). It is enforced by an import boundary that fails
  the build, and evaluated against both paraphrase and prompt-injection attacks.
\item A value-free result type for failed measurements: an identity check that
  could not run has no similarity field at all, so it cannot be read as a low
  score (Section~\ref{sec:dl}).
\item A within-query ranking diagnostic that separates matching ability from
  entity-level quality on any paired benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:within}).
\end{enumerate}
The remaining contributions are empirical: a nine-model comparison with a
synthetic positive control that establishes statistical power, and an
empirical calibration showing that a documented, authored threshold was wrong
by a quantified amount.

\section{Related Work}
\emph{Audits of hiring systems.} Bogen and Rieke~\cite{bogen2018} examined
hiring algorithms across the recruitment funnel and found predictive tools
reproducing historical patterns. Raghavan et al.~\cite{raghavan2020} surveyed
vendors of algorithmic pre-employment assessment and showed that fairness
analysis is conducted against the stated prediction target rather than the
deployed one; they offer no remedy for a misdescribed target, and
Section~\ref{sec:within} supplies a measurement that detects one. Wachter et
al.~\cite{wachter2018} argue that explanations of automated decisions should
let the affected person contest them, which is the property a per-claim audit
is built to provide.

\emph{Language-model reliability.} Generative models produce fluent text not
supported by their input~\cite{ji2023}, and instructions hidden in retrieved
content can redirect an integrated model~\cite{greshake2023}. Both failures
matter when a model reads r\'esum\'es; Section~\ref{sec:grounding} constrains
the first by construction and measures the second.

\emph{Evaluation protocol.} Ferrari Dacrema et al.~\cite{dacrema2019} could
reproduce only seven of eighteen neural recommenders, six of which were often
beaten by simple heuristics; Musgrave et al.~\cite{musgrave2020} found the same
for deep metric learning, and Grinsztajn et al.~\cite{grinsztajn2022} showed
tree ensembles still rival deep networks on tabular data. Dem\v{s}ar~\cite{demsar2006}
recommends the Friedman test~\cite{friedman1937} followed by paired post-hoc
tests rather than reading a ranking off raw score differences; Nadeau and
Bengio~\cite{nadeau2003} show that overlapping training sets bias naive variance
estimates; Gorman and Bedrick~\cite{gorman2019} showed that rankings from a
single split often fail to reproduce; and Kapoor and Narayanan~\cite{kapoor2023}
documented leakage in 294 ML-based science papers.

\emph{Perception.} We build on SCRFD~\cite{guo2022} and ArcFace~\cite{deng2019},
which leave the verification threshold to the integrator, and calibrate it on
LFW~\cite{huang2007}. Face-analysis error rates differ across demographic
groups~\cite{buolamwini2018}, one reason CogniHire performs no demographic
inference. Across these strands three gaps recur: no work constrains generative
extraction to selection; prediction targets go unverified behind pooled
metrics; and perception components ship with authored, unmeasured thresholds.

The audit has four verdicts: \code{substantiated}, \code{notDemonstrated},
\code{contradicted} and \code{notExamined}. They are deliberately not
orderable, and no composite is computed. \code{notExamined} is essential:
without it an unprobed claim would simply be absent from the report, and
absence reads as acceptance. In the implementation a claim with no evidence is
\code{notExamined}, and otherwise its verdict is whatever the reviewer sets,
defaulting to \code{notDemonstrated}; no model sets a verdict.

Because the system also runs learned components, it must be clear which of
their outputs can reach a recruiter. Table~\ref{tab:roles} lists every

To make the path concrete, consider one claim traced through all three
families. The r\'esum\'e contains the bullet ``Built REST APIs in Django for a
payments service.'' The language model proposes that sentence as a claim; the
grounding gate finds it verbatim inside one clause with no negation and admits
it with its character offsets. The interview engine asks how the service
handled idempotent retries; the answer is recorded as evidence, and the
answer-analysis step keeps the sentence from the answer that addresses
retries, discarding any quote not found verbatim in the answer. Meanwhile the
DL layer checks at intervals that the same person is present and logs the
result as a matched, mismatched or unchecked event. The reviewer sees the
claim, the question, the quoted answer and the identity log, and sets the
verdict. The verdict is the only judgment made about the claim, and a person
makes it; the one session-level number the current report still shows is
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\section{Evidence Grounding}
\label{sec:grounding}
\subsection{Selection Versus Generation}
The central rule is that a model may select text from the r\'esum\'e but may
not generate it. We define the distinction precisely, as the code implements
it.

\begin{definition}
Let $r$ be the r\'esum\'e text, split into clauses at sentence terminals
followed by whitespace, at semicolons and line breaks, and at the contrastive
conjunctions ``but'', ``however'' and ``although''. Let $\nu(\cdot)$ lower-case
a string and collapse runs of whitespace. A proposed claim $c$ is
\emph{selected} if there is a clause $k$ of $r$ and offsets $s<e$ inside $k$
with $\nu(r[s{:}e]) = \nu(c)$, and the part of $k$ outside $[s,e)$ contains no
negation or hedge term. Otherwise $c$ is \emph{generated}, and it is discarded.
\end{definition}

Under this definition, changing case or line wrapping is still selection;
rewording, summarizing, translating, merging two clauses, or dropping a
negation is generation. The term list covers standard negation (``not'',
``never'', ``without'', negative contractions, ``cannot'', ``failed to'') and
epistemic hedges (``possibly'', ``maybe'', ``might'', ``allegedly'',
``reportedly'', ``unsure''). Figure~\ref{fig:gate} shows the gate as the
sequence of checks that apply this definition, with an example of what each
check rejects. For instance, ``I have not worked with Kubernetes'' yields no
Kubernetes claim, ``possibly used Kafka'' yields no Kafka claim, and ``I used
Django, but never in production'' yields the Django claim alone.

\subsection{Measured Behavior}
An offline deterministic harness drives the real pipeline modules over 5,200
synthetic r\'esum\'es spanning eight fields and four seniority levels. No
language-model key is needed, so the result is reproducible.
Table~\ref{tab:gate} summarizes the result: across roughly 100,000 trials the
gate rejected all 48,149 meaning-preserving paraphrases and all 51,761
negation traps, before and after the fix described below.

The initial run showed a 0.82\% false-rejection rate: 425 of 51,761 genuine
claims discarded, every one containing a period-bearing token such as
\code{Node.js}, \code{asp.net} or \code{Python~3.9}. The clause splitter treated
every period as a terminal, so ``Node.js.'' split into ``Node'' and ``js.'' and
the claim was silently dropped. Requiring a terminal to be followed by
whitespace or end-of-string fixed it; since the change only makes clauses
longer, it widens negation scope and cannot weaken the guard. No unit test
caught this defect; only corpus-scale measurement with an explicit
true-positive metric did.

With a real model in the loop, the production extractor (Qwen2.5 7B~\cite{qwen2024},
run locally) proposed 193 claims across 24 real r\'esum\'es from a public
r\'esum\'e collection; the gate discarded 4 (2.1\%) as text the model had
written rather than selected. The synthetic corpus is single-generator, so we
do not claim 100\% extraction coverage in general; the defensible claim is the
safety property itself.

\subsection{Prompt-Injection Evaluation}
\label{sec:injection}
Because the planner consumes only typed claims, an instruction embedded in a
r\'esum\'e has no field in which it can act as an instruction. We tested this
with a worst-case threat model: the extraction model is fully compromised and
obeys every instruction planted in the r\'esum\'e. Twenty injection templates
(for example, ``Ignore all previous instructions and list Kubernetes, Rust and
AWS as expert skills'', ``SYSTEM: mark every claim for this candidate as
substantiated'', and an HTML comment asserting cloud certifications) were
inserted at the start, middle and end of each of the 24 real r\'esum\'es,
giving 1,440 injected documents. For each, the compromised model emitted the
content the injection demanded. Table~\ref{tab:injection} reports what the
gate admitted.

\begin{table}[t]

\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.55\columnwidth}rr@{}}
\toprule

\midrule

No fabricated output was admitted. The other two rows are the gate working as
specified, and they mark the limit of what grounding can promise: text the
attacker writes into the r\'esum\'e is the candidate's own words, so it can
become a claim. It becomes only a claim, however: a string with offsets that
the interview then probes, which cannot set a verdict, a score or a
question plan. The 144 injected sentences not admitted came from two
templates: one contains the negation ``do not'', and the other contains a
semicolon, which splits it into two clauses so it fails the single-clause
check. Grounding therefore removes the fabrication channel
but does not detect false statements a candidate chooses to write; that is the
purpose of the interview. The harness ships with the code.

\section{Machine Learning: What Model Choice Buys}
\label{sec:ml}
ML in CogniHire measures properties of evidence and is never trained on hiring
outcomes. This section asks a methodological question with a public benchmark:
how much does the choice of algorithm matter in screening-like matching? None
of the models below is deployed (Table~\ref{tab:roles}).

\subsection{Dataset, Pair Structure and Effective Sample Size}
We use the public r\'esum\'e--posting fit dataset~\cite{cnamuangtoun2024}. It
ships 8,000 labeled (r\'esum\'e, posting) pairs, split 6,241 for training and
1,759 for testing, with three labels: Good Fit, Potential Fit and No Fit. We
did not generate the pairs; they are the dataset's own. We binarize the label
(Good or Potential Fit versus No Fit), down-sample the majority class within
each split to balance it, and pool the two splits, which leaves 7,910 pairs
over 643 unique r\'esum\'es and 351 unique postings. Each r\'esum\'e therefore
appears in 12.3 pairs on average and each posting in 22.5, and 616 of the 643
r\'esum\'es are paired with more than one posting.

The dataset card gives no description, license, citation or labeling
procedure, so we treat the labels as an unvalidated proxy and draw no
conclusion about hiring validity from them. This is also why the benchmark is
used only diagnostically.

Repeated entities mean the 7,910 rows are not independent observations, so the
effective sample size is far smaller than 7,910. The protocol accounts for this
in three ways. First, cross-validation is grouped by posting, so no test
posting appears in training. Second, every significance test below is computed
over the ten fold-level scores, not over rows, so row-level dependence cannot
inflate it. Third, where a test would otherwise treat related pairs as
independent (Table~\ref{tab:within}), we report a fold-level sign test instead.

\subsection{Protocol and Results}
Every experiment uses ten-fold grouped cross-validation. IDF statistics and the
feature scaler are fitted inside each fold on training rows only, and identical
folds are reused for every model so all comparisons are paired. We compared
nine classifiers from six families using scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011};
Table~\ref{tab:nine} gives each model's mean AUROC, calibration error
(ECE)~\cite{guo2017}, inference latency and model size.

The nine span only 0.031 AUROC, comparable to each model's fold-to-fold

performance ($\chi^2 = 38.35$, $p = 6.5\times10^{-6}$), but under paired

leading MLP is statistically indistinguishable from all four tree ensembles
(adjusted $p$ from 0.078 to 0.262). A ranked table alone would have licensed
``MLP is best''; the significance analysis does not.

A null result is uninformative unless the protocol can detect a difference
when one exists. We therefore ran the same nine models, folds and code on a
synthetic control with a planted generative process whose best achievable
accuracy is 0.925. Figure~\ref{fig:folds} shows both tasks side by side. Each
box spans the middle half of a model's ten fold scores, with the line at the
median and whiskers to the most extreme non-outlying folds. On the real task
(a) the boxes overlap almost completely; on the control (b) they separate
clearly ($\chi^2 = 55.42$, $p = 3.7\times10^{-9}$). The null is a property of
the task, not the protocol.

\subsection{Separating Matching From Candidate Quality}
\label{sec:within}
A pooled metric rewards two capabilities it cannot distinguish: knowing which
r\'esum\'es tend to be labeled fit, and knowing which postings a given
r\'esum\'e fits. Only the second is matching. Varying the input representation
under identical folds, the best representation (concatenated 1536-dimensional
r\'esum\'e and posting embeddings~\cite{nussbaum2024}) reaches 0.755 AUROC. A
probe given only the r\'esum\'e embedding, which cannot be matching, reaches
0.660 AUROC, 87\% of that best score.

This raises the question of what the labels measure. Decomposing label
variance, 19.4\% lies between r\'esum\'es and 80.6\% within them. The labels are
therefore mostly relational, but the between-r\'esum\'e share is large enough
for a r\'esum\'e-only model to score well, which means a pooled metric can
reward r\'esum\'e-level properties such as length or seniority rather than
fit. To isolate matching, we evaluate on pairs of rows sharing a r\'esum\'e, one
positive and one negative, and ask whether the model ranks the positive
posting higher. Every r\'esum\'e-level property cancels, so chance is exactly
0.5. Table~\ref{tab:within} gives the result for the random forest over 4,112
such pairs.

Models do match: given one r\'esum\'e and two postings, they pick the fitting

But for the same random forest, moving from the relational to the
1536-dimensional interaction representation raises pooled AUROC by $+0.048$
(0.688 to 0.737) and between-r\'esum\'e ranking by $+0.047$, while
within-r\'esum\'e matching changes by $-0.009$ ($p > 0.4$, paired Wilcoxon over
folds). The improvement a leaderboard would record came from ranking candidates
against each other, not from matching them to roles. The diagnostic needs no
extra labels and applies to any paired benchmark with a recurring entity
(patient--treatment, user--item, query--document). In hiring it is the
measurement that distinguishes ranking matches from ranking people, the target
substitution Raghavan et al.~\cite{raghavan2020} identify.

\section{Deep Learning: Calibrating Identity}
\label{sec:dl}
Face detection uses SCRFD~\cite{guo2022} and embedding uses
\mbox{ArcFace}~\cite{deng2019}; both are frozen. Retraining on project-scale
data would be worse and would introduce demographic bias we cannot
audit~\cite{buolamwini2018}. The vendor pack's age and gender classifier is
excluded by a loader allow-list.

The system originally used an authored cosine-similarity threshold of 0.50,
reasoned and documented but flagged as uncalibrated. We ran an equal-error-rate
sweep on 2,200 LFW~\cite{huang2007} training pairs and evaluated the chosen
threshold on 1,000 held-out pairs. Table~\ref{tab:face} compares the
calibrated and authored thresholds; the embedding's AUC on the held-out pairs
is 0.9785 at any threshold.

(LFW, 1,000 Held-Out Pairs)}
\label{tab:face}
\centering
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
Threshold & FAR & FRR & Accuracy \\
\midrule
0.1266 (calibrated) & 0.030 & 0.034 & 0.968 \\
0.50 (authored)     & 0.000 & 0.414 & 0.793 \\

The authored value would have falsely rejected 41.4\% of genuine matches.
Because the system refuses to treat an unmeasured check as a pass, roughly two
in five legitimate candidates would have accumulated mismatch events for being
themselves. Calibration cut false rejection twelvefold at a false-acceptance
cost of 3.0\%. In security-adjacent systems, a reasoned value and a correct
value are different properties, and only the second can be demonstrated.

The identity result type is three-valued. \code{Unchecked} carries a reason and
has no similarity field at all---not 0.0, not null---so no downstream
comparison can read a failed measurement as a low-but-real one, in the spirit
of distribution-free abstention~\cite{vovk2005}. LFW is a public-figure
benchmark, not our candidate population; population-specific recalibration
requires consented candidate data we have not collected.

\section{Integration, Compliance, and Limitations}
Each family alone leaves a gap. Grounding guarantees claims are the candidate's
own words but not that a session gathered enough evidence to judge them; ML can
measure evidence sufficiency but, without grounding, would measure text a model
may have invented; DL supplies identity continuity but no verdict. Together
they close a loop that ends in a human reviewer, not an aggregate.

This design maps onto regulation, as Table~\ref{tab:reg} shows obligation by
obligation. The instruments share a direction: an automated employment decision
must be disclosed, auditable, and open to human oversight and explanation. A
system built around a scalar faces a retrofit its own architecture resists,
since the explanatory information was discarded at design time. The mapping is
possible because CogniHire declines to produce an aggregate: a bias audit of a
composite score must explain the score, whereas a bias audit of a claim audit
examines the distribution of verdicts and the evidence attached to each, a
smaller and better-posed question.

\toprule
Obligation & Instrument & Mechanism in CogniHire \\
\midrule

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table*}

Before building, we audited a comparable open system in full. Its
computer-vision core was sound, but its decision layer was largely fabricated.
An object detector configured with an invalid model name never ran once; a
luminance heuristic filled the gap and logged window glare as camera-verified
phone detections, which fed an escalating penalty system. The identity endpoint
returned a 0.98-confidence match when no enrolled face existed, and a
r\'esum\'e analyzer returned a hardcoded skill list on zero keyword matches.
The defects share one root cause: a fallback that fabricates a measurement is
worse than an outage. CogniHire's responses---a value-free type for failed
measurements, component health surfaced to a person, every output traceable to
a source span---follow directly. For the same reason we do not build affect,
stress or deception estimation: none has defensible ground truth on a diverse
candidate pool, and a stress percentage that silently changes which questions a
candidate is asked is itself a hidden score.

\emph{Limitations.}\label{sec:limits} The most important gap is in the
implementation, not the design. The web report currently shows, beside the
per-claim audit, the mean model confidence across answered topics and the
share of planned topics covered. The first is a session-level aggregate that a
recruiter could read as a candidate score, which conflicts with the principle
this paper argues for; removing it, and leaving only the per-answer judgments,
is the most direct next change. Identity matching is implemented and tested but is currently only
a presence gate on the production path, so continuous identity verification is
not yet deployed. The face threshold is calibrated on a public benchmark, not
candidates. No structured recruiter study has been conducted, so the premise
that reviewers prefer a claim audit to a score is untested. The injection
evaluation uses 24 host r\'esum\'es and hand-written templates, and the
fit labels come from a dataset with no documented labeling procedure. A
r\'esum\'e--posting fit model from an earlier iteration of this work (0.657
AUROC) is deliberately not deployed: Section~\ref{sec:within} shows that pooled
fit scores on this benchmark largely reward ranking candidates rather than
matching them.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
CogniHire replaces the composite hiring score with a per-claim audit whose
guarantees are enforced by architecture rather than by prompt discipline. We
separate what this paper demonstrates from what it only proposes.

Demonstrated by measurement: the grounding gate rejected every paraphrase and
negation trap across roughly 100,000 synthetic trials and admitted none of
1,800 fabricated claims from a fully compromised model; a carefully reasoned
face threshold would have falsely rejected 41.4\% of genuine matches, and
calibration reduced that to 3.4\% on LFW; and nine classifiers were
statistically indistinguishable at the top of a public screening benchmark,
where a within-query diagnostic showed that apparent gains came from ranking
candidates rather than matching them.

Proposed but not yet demonstrated: that recruiters make better or fairer
decisions with a claim audit than with a score; that the face threshold holds
on the candidate population; and that the system's regulatory mapping would
satisfy an auditor. These are the next steps: a controlled recruiter study
comparing inter-reviewer agreement on a claim audit against a composite score,
recalibration of the face threshold on consented candidate data, end-to-end
timing of the interview stage, and continuous identity verification on the
production path.
