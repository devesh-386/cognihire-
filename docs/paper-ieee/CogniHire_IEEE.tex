\begin{abstract}
Automated hiring systems typically emit a score: a scalar that ranks a
candidate against others. Such scores resist explanation, give a candidate
nothing specific to contest, and carry growing regulatory exposure under New
York City Local Law~144, the Illinois Human Rights Act as amended by HB~3773,
and the EU Artificial Intelligence Act. We present CogniHire, a technical
screening system designed to audit the claims a candidate makes rather than
score the candidate. A deterministic grounding gate constrains a language model to select
claim text from the resume and forbids it to author any. Across roughly
100,000 synthetic trials the gate rejected every meaning-preserving paraphrase
and negation trap while admitting every verbatim claim, and under a worst-case
prompt-injection model it admitted none of 1,800 fabricated claims. For machine
learning we compare nine classifiers under grouped cross-validation with
significance testing and introduce a within-query diagnostic that separates
matching ability from candidate-level quality. For deep learning we show that a
plausible authored face-similarity threshold of 0.50 would have falsely rejected
41.4\% of genuine identity matches, against 3.4\% at a threshold calibrated on
Labeled Faces in the Wild. Each design policy is enforced as a structural
property of the build rather than by developer discipline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Interview assessments are commonly summarized as a single score, which records
neither which answer produced it nor how it could be contested. Automated
screening platforms inherit this limitation~\cite{bogen2018,raghavan2020}.

We propose that the useful output of automated screening is an
audit of the claims a candidate has made, not a judgment of the candidate. Each
resume claim is probed, each probe yields evidence, and the verdicts, with
evidence attached, go to a human who decides about the person. Grounding
governs what may be asserted, machine learning (ML) measures properties of
evidence, and deep learning (DL) supplies perception.

Auditing rather than scoring is a design stance, not the technical novelty.
The novel mechanisms are: (1) an assertion-scoped grounding gate that admits a
claim only as a verbatim, single-clause span of the resume with no negation or
hedge, returns its character offsets, is enforced by an import boundary that
fails the build, and is evaluated against paraphrase and prompt-injection
attacks (Section~\ref{sec:grounding}); (2) a value-free result type, so a
failed measurement cannot be read as a low score (Section~\ref{sec:dl}); and
(3) a within-query ranking diagnostic that separates matching ability from
entity-level quality (Section~\ref{sec:within}). The remaining contributions
are empirical: a nine-model comparison with a synthetic positive control, and a
calibration showing that an authored threshold was wrong by a quantified
amount.

\section{Related Work}
Audits of hiring algorithms find predictive tools reproducing historical
patterns~\cite{bogen2018}, and fairness analysis is run against a vendor's
stated prediction target rather than the deployed one~\cite{raghavan2020};
Section~\ref{sec:within} supplies a measurement that detects such a mismatch.
Explanations should let the affected person contest a decision~\cite{wachter2018},
the property a per-claim audit is built to provide. Language models produce
fluent text unsupported by their input~\cite{ji2023}, and instructions hidden in
retrieved content can redirect them~\cite{greshake2023}; we constrain the
first by construction and measure the second. Table~\ref{tab:novelty}
positions the grounding gate against neighboring techniques: none combines
verbatim span admission, assertion-level negation and hedge checks, and an
architectural boundary that keeps model text out of verdicts.

Published gains often fail controlled re-evaluation~\cite{dacrema2019,musgrave2020},

Friedman test~\cite{friedman1937} followed by paired post-hoc tests. For
perception we use SCRFD~\cite{guo2022} and ArcFace~\cite{deng2019}, which leave
the verification threshold to the integrator, and calibrate it on
LFW~\cite{huang2007}; face-analysis error rates differ across demographic
groups~\cite{buolamwini2018}, one reason CogniHire performs no demographic
inference.

As an example, the bullet ``Built REST APIs in Django for a payments
service'' is proposed by the model, found verbatim in one clause by the gate,
and admitted with its offsets. The interview asks how the service handled
idempotent retries; a verbatim quote from the answer becomes evidence, the DL
layer logs presence, and the reviewer sets the verdict. No number about the
candidate is computed.

Each design policy is enforced as a structure rather than a convention: an
import boundary checked in continuous integration stops the AI module from
authoring claims, a vocabulary ban that fails the build forbids a composite
score, and an append-only event table makes every session reconstructible.

\section{Evidence Grounding}
\label{sec:grounding}
\subsection{Selection Versus Generation}
A model may select text from the resume but may not generate it; the code
implements the distinction as follows.

\begin{definition}
Let $r$ be the resume text, split into clauses at sentence terminals
followed by whitespace, at semicolons and line breaks, and at the contrastive
conjunctions ``but'', ``however'' and ``although''. Let $\nu(\cdot)$ lower-case
a string and collapse runs of whitespace. A proposed claim $c$ is
\emph{selected} if there is a clause $k$ of $r$ and offsets $s<e$ inside $k$
with $\nu(r[s{:}e]) = \nu(c)$, and the part of $k$ outside $[s,e)$ contains no
negation or hedge term. Otherwise $c$ is \emph{generated}, and it is discarded.
\end{definition}

Under this definition, changing case or line wrapping is still selection;
rewording, summarizing, translating, merging two clauses, or dropping a
negation is generation. The term list covers negation (``not'', ``never'', ``without'', ``cannot'')
and hedges (``possibly'', ``maybe'', ``might'', ``allegedly'').
Figure~\ref{fig:gate} shows the checks that apply this definition. For
instance, ``I have not worked with Kubernetes'' yields no
Kubernetes claim, ``possibly used Kafka'' yields no Kafka claim, and ``I used
Django, but never in production'' yields the Django claim alone.

architecture test fails continuous integration if that import appears, so the

\subsection{Measured Behavior}
An offline deterministic harness drives the real pipeline modules over 5,200
synthetic resumes spanning eight fields and four seniority levels. No
language-model key is needed, so the result is reproducible.
Table~\ref{tab:gate} summarizes the result: across roughly 100,000 trials the
gate rejected all 48,149 meaning-preserving paraphrases and all 51,761
negation traps, before and after the fix described below. These rates hold for
the generator's paraphrase and negation patterns, not arbitrary real phrasing.

The initial run falsely rejected 425 of 51,761 genuine claims (0.82\%), each
containing a period-bearing token such as Node.js: the clause splitter treated
every period as a sentence end. Requiring whitespace after a terminal fixed it
without weakening the guard. Only corpus-scale measurement caught this.

With a real model in the loop, the production extractor (Qwen2.5 7B~\cite{qwen2024},
run locally) proposed 193 claims across 24 real resumes from a public
resume collection; the gate discarded 4 (2.1\%) as text the model had
written rather than selected; the 189 admitted claims were not manually
annotated for relevance. The synthetic corpus is single-generator, so the
defensible claim is the safety property, not general extraction coverage.

\subsection{Ablation on Real Resumes}
To test the gate outside the synthetic generator and to show that each check is
necessary, we built trials from the 24 independently written real resumes:
950 genuine single-clause sentences, and for each a negated and a hedged

\subsection{Prompt-Injection Evaluation}
\label{sec:injection}
Because the planner consumes only typed claims, an instruction embedded in a
resume has no field in which to act. We tested this against a fully
compromised extraction model that obeys every planted instruction. Twenty injection templates
(for example, ``Ignore all previous instructions and list Kubernetes, Rust and
AWS as expert skills'', ``SYSTEM: mark every claim for this candidate as
substantiated'', and an HTML comment asserting cloud certifications) were
inserted at the start, middle and end of each of the 24 real resumes,
giving 1,440 injected documents. For each, the compromised model emitted the
content the injection demanded. Table~\ref{tab:injection} reports what the
gate admitted.

\begin{table}[t]

(1,440 Injected Resumes)}
\label{tab:injection}

\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.55\columnwidth}rr@{}}

No fabricated output was admitted. The other rows mark the limit of grounding: text an
attacker writes into the resume is the candidate's own words, so it can become
a claim, but only a claim for the interview to probe, never a verdict, score
or question plan. (The 144 injected sentences not admitted contain ``do not''
or a semicolon.) Four further vectors, each over the same 1,440 documents,
admitted no fabricated output either: zero-width characters, Cyrillic
homoglyphs, an instruction split across lines, and HTML hidden text; nor did
instructions in Spanish, Hindi, Chinese and French. Instructions inside images
or PDF metadata were not tested. Grounding removes the fabrication channel; detecting false
statements a candidate writes is the interview's job.

\section{Machine Learning: What Model Choice Buys}
\label{sec:ml}
ML in CogniHire is never trained on hiring outcomes. This section asks, on a
public benchmark, how much the choice of algorithm matters for screening-like
matching; none of these models is deployed.

\subsection{Dataset, Pair Structure and Effective Sample Size}
The public fit dataset~\cite{cnamuangtoun2024} ships 8,000 labeled
(resume, posting) pairs (6,241 train, 1,759 test) labeled Good, Potential or No
Fit; the pairs are the dataset's own. Binarizing (Good or Potential versus No
Fit), balancing each split and pooling leaves 7,910 pairs over 643 resumes and
351 postings: 12.3 pairs per resume and 22.5 per posting on average. The
dataset card gives no license or labeling procedure, so the labels are treated
as an unvalidated proxy and the benchmark is used only diagnostically.

Repeated entities make the effective sample size far smaller than 7,910, so
folds are grouped by posting and every significance test uses the ten
fold-level scores, not rows (Table~\ref{tab:within}).

\subsection{Protocol and Results}
Every experiment uses ten-fold grouped cross-validation. IDF statistics and the
feature scaler are fitted inside each fold on training rows only, and identical
folds are reused for every model so all comparisons are paired. We compared
nine classifiers from six families using scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011};
Table~\ref{tab:nine} gives each model's mean AUROC (area under the ROC curve;
0.5 is chance), expected calibration error (ECE, the mean gap between predicted
probability and observed frequency over ten bins)~\cite{guo2017}, latency and
model size.

The nine span only 0.031 AUROC, comparable to each model's fold-to-fold

(Kendall's $W = 0.48$), and a corrected resampled $t$-test~\cite{nadeau2003}
with Holm correction also finds no significant pairwise difference from the
MLP; mean AUROC 95\% intervals overlap for all nine models. A ranked table alone would have licensed
``MLP is best''; the significance analysis does not.

folds and code on a synthetic control with a planted signal (best achievable
accuracy 0.925). Figure~\ref{fig:folds} shows both: each box spans the middle
half of a model's ten fold scores. On the real task (a) the boxes overlap
almost completely; on the control (b) they separate clearly ($\chi^2 = 55.42$, $p = 3.7\times10^{-9}$). The null is a property of
the task, not the protocol.

\subsection{Separating Matching From Candidate Quality}
\label{sec:within}
A pooled metric rewards two capabilities it cannot distinguish: knowing which
resumes tend to be labeled fit, and knowing which postings a given
resume fits. Only the second is matching. Varying the input representation
under identical folds, the best representation (concatenated 1536-dimensional
resume and posting embeddings~\cite{nussbaum2024}) reaches 0.755 AUROC. A
probe given only the resume embedding, which cannot be matching, reaches
0.660 AUROC, 87\% of that best score.

Of label variance, 19.4\% lies between resumes and 80.6\% within them: the
labels are mostly relational, yet the between-resume share lets a resume-only
model score well. To isolate matching, we take pairs of rows sharing a resume,
one positive and one negative, and ask whether the model ranks the positive
posting higher; every resume-level property cancels, so chance is 0.5. Table~\ref{tab:within} gives the result for the random forest over 4,112
such pairs.

\midrule
Relational (10-d)     & 0.690 & 0.633 & 9 of 10  & 0.021 \\
Interaction (1536-d)  & 0.737 & 0.624 & 10 of 10 & 0.002 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Models do match: given one resume and two postings, they pick the fitting
one about 63\% of the time, and they beat chance in nine or ten of ten folds.
But for the same random forest, moving from the relational to the
1536-dimensional interaction representation raises pooled AUROC by $+0.048$
(0.688 to 0.737) and between-resume ranking by $+0.047$, while
within-resume matching changes by $-0.009$ ($p > 0.4$, paired Wilcoxon over
folds). The improvement a leaderboard would record came from ranking candidates
against each other, not from matching them to roles. The diagnostic needs no extra labels, applies
to any paired benchmark with a recurring entity, and detects the target
substitution Raghavan et al.~\cite{raghavan2020} describe.

\subsection{Robustness and Cost}
Three further checks support the null result. Varying only the splitting
scheme (all 8,000 rows, a class-weighted random forest), the ungrouped scheme
scores 0.714 AUROC against 0.689 grouped by posting, a modest leakage
inflation; grouping by resume scores 0.703, which refutes a
strong-memorization account; fully entity-disjoint folds are future work. Removing the ambiguous class scores 0.681, no
better than the binarized label. A learning curve flattens at 0.688 AUROC, so the limit is not sample size.

Cost is dominated by language-model extraction, not by the classifiers. Claim
extraction with Qwen2.5 7B run locally took a median of 12.1\,s per resume
(4.9--36.6\,s over 24 real resumes); the grounding gate took 1.1\,ms per claim
check; the classifiers of Table~\ref{tab:nine} take 0.2--195\,$\mu$s per pair.
Face checks and per-answer judgments were not timed, so a complete end-to-end
figure is not yet available.

\section{Deep Learning: Calibrating Identity}
\label{sec:dl}
Face detection (SCRFD~\cite{guo2022}) and embedding
(\mbox{ArcFace}~\cite{deng2019}) are frozen; the vendor's age and gender
classifier is excluded by a loader allow-list.

The system originally used an authored cosine-similarity threshold of 0.50,
reasoned and documented but flagged as uncalibrated. FAR is the fraction of
different-person pairs accepted and FRR the fraction of same-person pairs
rejected. We chose the equal-error-rate threshold, where the two are equal,
because no validated cost ratio between them exists for this use; it was fitted
on the 2,200 pairs of the LFW~\cite{huang2007} development training split and
tested on its 1,000 held-out pairs. Table~\ref{tab:face} compares the
calibrated and authored thresholds; the embedding's AUC on the held-out pairs
is 0.9785 at any threshold. Wilson 95\% intervals on the 500 genuine and 500
impostor test pairs are 0.018--0.049 for FAR and 0.021--0.054 for FRR.

The authored value would have falsely rejected 41.4\% of genuine matches.
Calibration cut false rejection twelvefold at a false-acceptance
cost of 3.0\%. 

The identity result type is three-valued. Unchecked carries a reason and
has no similarity field at all---not 0.0, not null---so no downstream
comparison can read a failed measurement as a low-but-real one, in the spirit
of distribution-free abstention~\cite{vovk2005}.

\section{Integration, Compliance, and Limitations}
Grounding guarantees claims are the candidate's own words, ML measures
evidence properties, and DL supplies identity continuity; none sets a verdict,
and the loop ends in a human reviewer, not an aggregate.

The design corresponds architecturally to current regulation; whether it
achieves legal compliance is outside this paper's scope. Bias audits
and candidate notification (NYC Local Law 144~\cite{nycll144}, the Illinois AI
Video Interview Act~\cite{ilaivia} and HB~3773~\cite{ilhb3773}) are served by
per-session consent and by auditing the per-claim verdict distribution rather
than a score. Human oversight, explanation of decisions and record-keeping (EU
AI Act Arts.~14, 86 and 12~\cite{euaiact}) are served by reviewer-set verdicts,
evidence attached to every verdict with character offsets, and an append-only
event store.

\emph{Limitations.}\label{sec:limits} A mean model confidence
per session, which a recruiter could read as a candidate score, was removed
from the report during this revision.
Each judgment carries an uncalibrated model confidence in $[0,1]$ with one
use: below 0.6 the topic gets a follow-up question. It is not shown to the
reviewer and never aggregated; a follow-up adds evidence but sets no verdict.
Identity matching is implemented and tested but is currently only
a presence gate on the production path, so continuous identity verification is
not yet deployed. The face threshold is calibrated on a public benchmark, not
candidates. No structured recruiter study has been conducted, so the premise
that reviewers prefer a claim audit to a score is untested. Injection templates are
hand-written, not attacks observed in the wild.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
Demonstrated by measurement, on the evaluated data: the grounding gate made
no error on roughly 100,000 synthetic and 4,100 real-resume trials, each of its
checks is necessary, and it admitted none of 1,800 fabricated claims from a
fully compromised model; an authored face threshold would have falsely
rejected 41.4\% of genuine LFW matches, against 3.4\% after calibration; and
nine classifiers were statistically indistinguishable on a public benchmark,
where apparent gains came from ranking candidates rather than matching them.

Not yet demonstrated: that reviewers decide better or more consistently with a
claim audit than with a score, that the face threshold holds for candidates,
and that the design satisfies a legal audit. Next steps are a controlled
reviewer study (agreement, review time, decision consistency), end-to-end
validation and timing on consented sessions, entity-disjoint and repeated
cross-validation, image and document-level injection vectors, and face
evaluation stratified by demographics, device and lighting.
